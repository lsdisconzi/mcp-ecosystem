Executive Summary — Disconzi v. LATAM, DGAC, PDI and the 4th Family Court of Santiago Overview
Case: I-002 Incident: 5 July 2024 Jurisdiction: Chile · Brazil · UK · US Status: Evidence Preserved

What This Case Is About

On July 5, 2024, Leandro Disconzi — a Brazilian and Italian citizen, resident in Chile, with 20 years of prior life in the United Kingdom — was forcibly removed from a LATAM flight at Arturo Merino Benítez Airport in Santiago.

The reason given: an allegation of assault against a LATAM employee.

That allegation was false.

The Chilean Investigations Police (PDI) reviewed the security footage. It found nothing. The PDI dismissed both allegations made against the passenger. No complaint was filed against him.

Despite this, the passenger:

  • Missed his flight to Brasília.
  • Missed his bus connection (an additional 700 km).
  • Was physically removed from the aircraft by six people.
  • Was threatened with being “pushed” if he did not cooperate.
  • Was classified as a “disruptive passenger” — a designation that prevents him from flying.
  • Was banned from flying with LATAM within 24 hours to full ban
  • Received no written explanation for his removal for more than 10 hours.
  • No remedy or compensation for the disembarkation document that the airline had filed with the DGAC and PDI.
  • Eventually received a document at the end of the day that was unsigned, unstamped, and used the wrong template, containing the same allegations that the PDI had already discredited.

The Institutional Response

The passenger documented everything — 23 audio recordings, 29 verified emails, and forensic transcripts. The recordings capture:

  • LATAM personnel admitting that this happens “every day” (“passa diario”) and that “the airline always wins with the police.”
  • Carabineros confirming the systemic pattern: “Almost every day… they always do that.”
  • The DGAC admitting that its “hands are tied” — that it cannot ask why the airline wants to remove a passenger.
  • The PDI acknowledging that the accusation was a lie: “He is crying… that I hit you, a lie.”
  • The DGAC confirming that the disembarkation document exists but is “for the captain and for the DGAC” — not for the passenger.
  • A LATAM supervisor orchestrating the creation of a generic letter written by people who “nobody was present at the event” — that is, by people who did not witness anything.

The Family Context

The passenger was in Chile because his daughter — Olivia, born in Edinburgh and holding four nationalities — lives there. Olivia’s maternal grandfather is the administrative head of the same family court handling the custody and child-support cases.

The same court that:

  • Does not respond to the father’s submissions.
  • Issued an arrest warrant against him.
  • Allowed the maternal grandparents to unilaterally suspend video calls between father and daughter.

The grandparents admitted in writing: “The two previous times we forced her” — referring to forced video calls with a child who “did not want to talk.”

The Financial Consequences — Corrected & Current

The First Round: BNDES, FNAC, and the Share Buyback Contradiction (2026)

Earlier this month, Brazil's Minister of Ports and Airports, Tome Franca, announced with evident pride that BNDES had approved access to R$8 billion from the National Civil Aviation Fund (FNAC) for airlines operating in the domestic market — Azul, Gol, LATAM, and Abaeté. The resources, available until December 2026, are earmarked for "working capital" to provide liquidity amidst "strong cost pressures," particularly the recent rise in aviation fuel prices.

The announcement was framed as a victory for Brazilian aviation — preserving jobs, maintaining the national air network, strengthening regional connectivity, and ensuring Brazilians continue to have access to air transport.

But this framing demands scrutiny, because the contradiction is not theoretical. It is happening right now.

The Core Contradiction: Subsidized Credit for Companies Distributing Cash to Shareholders

The financing carries a heavily subsidized annual interest rate of 4% — a condition far more favorable than lines normally available to other Brazilian companies. The stated justification is that these airlines need this liquidity to survive cost pressures.

Yet the question that immediately arises is: what are these same companies doing with their own cash?

LATAM, the largest beneficiary of this public credit, has a well-documented history of using its cash for share buybacks — repurchasing its own stock on the Santiago Stock Exchange, at times representing a staggering percentage of daily market volume. This is not ancient history; it is a pattern of capital allocation that continues.

The economic logic is inescapable. When BNDES provides R$8 billion in cheap, subsidized credit for "working capital," it frees up the company's own cash reserves. That freed-up cash can then be redirected to activities that enrich shareholders — buybacks, dividends — rather than the operational resilience the public financing is supposed to achieve.

This is funds displacement, and it represents a fundamental inversion of public policy: the Brazilian state is effectively subsidizing shareholder returns while being told the airlines need help to survive.

The Silence on Oversight

The Minister's response to questions about oversight is telling. When asked directly about how this "noble investment" would be monitored to prevent diversion, the response was: "It is a reimbursable bank financing, not a transfer of resources. BNDES performs technical analysis, requires guarantees, monitors the operation, and the control bodies oversee the entire process. The resources will be fully returned by the companies."

This is not an answer. It is a dodge.

Saying "it's a loan, not a grant" does not address the question of how the public can verify that these resources are actually achieving the stated goals of maintaining the air network, preserving jobs, and ensuring connectivity. It does not explain:

  • What specific criteria BNDES uses in its technical analysis.
  • What indicators are monitored, and whether reports are public.
  • Which control bodies are involved, and how their oversight functions in practice.
  • Where society can access this information.

The fact that the money will be "returned" is irrelevant to the question of whether it is being used appropriately while in the airlines' possession. A subsidized loan at 4% — far below market rates — is itself a public benefit. The question is what the public receives in return.

The Regulatory Agenda: The Same Companies, the Same Moment

This is where the contradiction becomes institutional.

The R$8 billion in subsidized credit is being made available at the precise moment when the same airlines — through their industry association and their advocates within the regulatory apparatus — are lobbying to weaken consumer protections in ANAC's proposed revision of Resolução 400.

The narrative being pushed is that Brazilian passengers engage in "predatory litigation" — that the courts are clogged with frivolous lawsuits, and that airlines need regulatory relief from the burden of accountability.

But the evidence points to a very different story. An analysis of 2,013 real court rulings against LATAM, GOL, and AZUL reveals:

72.4% of merit rulings favor the passenger
83% of cases stem from operational or commercial failures
R$10,000 median award — hardly a litigation industry

The real "predatory litigation" is corporate. It is the strategy of systematically failing to comply with legal obligations, forcing passengers into court for compensation they should have received voluntarily, and then using political influence to try to change the rules rather than the behavior.

The Institutional Shield: ANAC's Jurisdictional Contradiction

The pattern of institutional protection extends beyond finance.

When a Brazilian passenger, Leandro Disconzi, was forcibly removed from a LATAM flight in Santiago, Chile, falsely accused of assault, and banned for life — despite official Chilean police confirming via CCTV that no aggression occurred — he filed a formal denunciation with ANAC.

ANAC's response was to decline jurisdiction, arguing that the incident occurred "entirely at Santiago Airport, on soil subject to the jurisdiction of the Chilean civil aviation authority."

This is the same ANAC that claims jurisdiction to rewrite liability rules for the entire Brazilian aviation market. It cannot have it both ways. If ANAC has the authority to weaken consumer protections for millions of Brazilian passengers, it certainly has the authority to investigate a complaint from a Brazilian citizen against a Brazilian-regulated carrier, for a ticket purchased in Brazil.

The correspondence between the passenger and ANAC's President, Tiago Faierstein, reveals a pattern that is all too familiar: promises to investigate, months of silence, and finally a reassertion of the same evasive conclusion. The agency that is supposed to regulate LATAM appears instead to be operating as an extension of its public relations department.

Conclusion: The Cost Is Socialized, the Benefit Is Privatized

This is not a series of isolated incidents. It is a coherent system:

  1. The state provides R$8 billion in subsidized credit, justified by the need to preserve jobs and connectivity.
  2. The airlines redirect their freed-up cash to shareholder payouts, not operational resilience.
  3. The industry fails operationally, violating consumer rights on a massive scale.
  4. The industry exploits the resulting lawsuits to build a false narrative of "predatory litigation."
  5. The industry lobbies the regulator to weaken the very rules being broken.
  6. The regulator complies, using jurisdictional arguments to shield the industry from accountability.

The real financial consequence is not the R$8 billion. It is the systemic transfer of risk and cost from private companies to the Brazilian public — who finance the airlines through subsidized credit, who pay for their failures through degraded service, and who are then blamed for seeking justice.

The question is not whether the money will be repaid. It is whether the Brazilian public will ever receive what it was promised in return.

  5. A financial contradiction: subsidized government financing while shares are being repurchased — public money benefiting shareholders, not victims.
  6. An institutional conflict of interest: the passenger’s daughter’s grandfather is the administrative head of the court handling his case.

Defined Terms & Terminology Applied in This Overview Glossary

This overview applies a revised legal vocabulary. The composite concepts below name recurring institutional patterns and are referenced, in bold, where they first arise in the narratives that follow; the terminology refinements replace colloquial labels with more precise legal terms throughout.

Composite Concepts

Procedural Entrapment. A system in which a passenger's reasonable resistance to an unfounded removal order is converted into the legal basis for the removal itself. Application: how the "desobediencia" classification was applied after the baseless allegation.

Documentary Asymmetry. The imbalance in access to records: institutions hold the documents (carta de desembarque, internal emails); the passenger does not. Application: underpins the systemic denial of access to information.

Institutional Passivity Doctrine. The position that a regulatory body (DGAC) cannot or will not investigate the underlying basis for an airline's removal decision. Application: captures the "atados de mano" admission.

Pretext Shifting. The practice of substituting new justifications for an action when previous ones are disproven. Application: the successive justifications for the removal.

Retaliatory Escalation. The pattern of increasing punitive measures (removal → travel ban → penal complaint) in response to the victim's assertion of rights. Application: the escalation from removal to the "Categoría 3" classification.

Status-Based Institutional Depersonalisation. The linguistic and procedural reduction of a passenger to a "normal citizen" — stripping the specific rights attached to passenger status. Application: the "disruptivo"/"ciudadano normal" language and its effect.
